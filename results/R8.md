# R8 — step 2: the loop pays to 16 applications, and beats eight times the parameters

**Status:** complete, one seed except where stated; the pod is gone. Read section
5b before anything else: three seeds each, and on the test split the causal control beats
the parallel decode by five points, reliably; on the world neither trained on the
parallel decode's three seeds sit within four points of each other while the
control's span twenty-one, and its mean is five points lower. The failures on the
unseen world are binding failures in both arms. The rest of this file is the loop
sweep. The step-2 gate passes, and by more than the first four
points suggested: goal success rises with the loop count at fixed parameters all
the way to 16 applications, +6.8 points, and a 1.05M-parameter block looped 16
times beats an unlooped block with eight times the parameters. The same runs found
a second lever worth as much — the sampler's commit schedule is worth +3.3 points
*and* a twelvefold cut in forward passes. One seed throughout.

**Two corrections to an earlier reading of this same data, recorded because the
mistake is instructive.** At L=8 the cosine curve gained only 0.3 points, which
looked like saturation, and the unlooped 8-layer block was ahead; both conclusions
were drawn before L=16 existed. L=16 then gained 2.0 points under the cosine
schedule and 3.2 under the confidence schedule, and it beat the 8x-parameter
block. A single flat increment on a single seed is not saturation. L=32, which was
cut on the strength of that flat increment, is being run.

Pending: L=32, two parameter-by-loop cells (4 and 8 layers looped 4 times), the
control arm at L=8, and the loop-index and effort-dial arms.

**Date:** 2026-09-18. **Plan:** `.claude/plans/npu-native-planner.md`, sequence
step 2, and bet 2 ("depth substitutes for width"). **Code:** `models/tiny/`,
`run_step2.sh`. **Model:** the step-1 architecture with the decoder stack shrunk
to one layer and applied L times with the same weights, so the parameter count is
identical at every L (`test_quant.py` asserts that). Width 256, 12 epochs, batch
64, `pad_weight` 0.5, seed 0, on the step-1 pod. **Data and scorer:** unchanged
from step 1 — `s5_plain`, the same 90/5/5 split, the same held-out world, every
program compiled by `core.pipeline.build` and executed by `harness.run.run_task`.

**Why a one-layer block rather than step 1's four.** Step 1's model reaches 98.2%
goal on the test split, so a loop sweep on it would measure the ceiling. A
one-layer block has room to move and is also the shape the NPU wants: the smaller
the block, the more loops fit in the same 4 MB of memory tiles.

## 1. The gate

Under the cosine schedule the step-1 runs used, k=8, test split:

| block | loops | applications per pass | block params | compile | goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 layer | 1 | 1 | 1.05M | 85.1% | 79.7% |
| 1 layer | 2 | 2 | 1.05M | 89.0% | 85.1% |
| 1 layer | 4 | 4 | 1.05M | 90.2% | 86.5% |
| 1 layer | 8 | 8 | 1.05M | 90.7% | 86.8% |
| 1 layer | 16 | 16 | 1.05M | 92.8% | 88.8% |
| 2 layers | 1 | 2 | 2.10M | 89.6% | 83.9% |
| 4 layers | 1 | 4 | 4.20M | 89.6% | 85.9% |
| 8 layers | 1 | 8 | 8.40M | 91.5% | 87.6% |

**The gate passes.** Goal success rises with the loop count at identical
parameters, +9.1 points from L=1 to L=16.

**It does not saturate by 16.** The increments are +5.4, +1.4, +0.3, +2.0. The
+0.3 at L=8 is the one that looked like a ceiling and was not; on one seed an
increment that small is noise, and the honest statement is that the curve is still
climbing where this sweep stops.

**At equal compute, looping beats parameters.** Each ladder rung does the same
arithmetic per pass as the loop count beside it, and the looped block wins at
every rung: +1.2 points at 2 applications against half its parameters, +0.6 at 4
against a quarter, +1.2 at 8 against an eighth. Decision 2's "looping is the whole
game on a resident block" is what these rows say.

**A consistency check worth stating.** The ladder's top rung is step 1's exact
configuration retrained by the step-2 harness, and it lands at 85.9% against step
1's 86.1% for the same seed. The sweep reproduces the earlier number, so it is
measuring what it claims.

## 2. The schedule, which turned out to matter more

Every row above pays exactly 8 forward passes, because the cosine schedule commits
a fixed number of slots per pass whether or not the model is sure of them. Step
1's k-sweep had already shown that the pass count is what moves the hardest level
(compound filter predicates: 0% goal at k=1, 16% at k=32) while this sweep shows
loops and parameters barely touch it. So what a pass buys is not refinement, it is
**commit granularity**: at 32 passes over a 64-slot canvas the sampler commits
about two slots per pass, each seeing the last.

Paying that on every program is waste, because most programs have nothing hard in
them. `sample.diffusion_sample --threshold` commits instead every unfilled slot
the model is at least that confident of, and at least one so it cannot stall, with
`--steps` as a cap. Step 1's diffusion checkpoint, full test split:

| schedule | mean passes | compile | goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| cosine, 8 steps | 8.0 | 90.7% | 86.1% |
| cosine, 8 steps + compiler repair | 8.6 | 94.1% | 88.2% |
| cosine, 32 steps | 32.0 | 94.8% | 88.9% |
| **threshold 0.99** | **2.7** | **97.1%** | **89.4%** |
| threshold 0.99 + compiler repair | 2.9 | 97.2% | 89.5% |

Better than 32 cosine steps at a twelfth of the passes, and better than the
compiler in the sampling loop without needing the compiler. It is ahead at every
level, most where the cosine schedule was weakest: level 3 (a compound predicate)
1% → 16% → 18% across the first, third and fourth rows, level 5 (IF/ELSE) 48% →
67% → 71%. Most programs finish in one or two passes; a compound predicate takes
about fifteen. That is compute proportional to difficulty, which is what a decode
on this NPU should look like.

It holds on the unseen world too: 82.5% → 85.8% goal at 8.0 → 2.9 passes on the
held-out world for the same checkpoint.

## 3. The loop curve again, under the better schedule

The question the schedule raises is whether the loop was only compensating for a
bad decode. It was not. Same checkpoints, threshold 0.99, cap 32, test split:

| block | loops | block params | passes | compile | goal | levels 0–6 | level 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 layer | 1 | 1.05M | 4.7 | 93.7% | 85.7% | 71.4% | 6.0% |
| 1 layer | 2 | 1.05M | 3.4 | 96.0% | 89.0% | 76.3% | 3.0% |
| 1 layer | 4 | 1.05M | 2.9 | 97.7% | **89.9%** | 78.1% | 11.9% |
| 1 layer | 8 | 1.05M | 2.7 | 97.1% | 89.3% | 76.3% | 14.9% |
| 1 layer | 16 | 1.05M | 2.3 | 98.8% | **92.5%** | **84.0%** | 34.3% |
| 2 layers | 1 | 2.10M | 3.3 | 95.1% | 88.1% | 75.2% | 9.0% |
| 8 layers | 1 | 8.40M | 2.2 | 99.4% | 91.4% | 82.1% | 34.3% |

Four things, and the last is the one the design turns on.

**The loop survives the better decode.** +6.8 points from one application to
sixteen, so it was not compensating for a bad schedule. The two levers add rather
than substitute.

**The loop also buys passes.** 4.7 → 3.4 → 2.9 → 2.7 → 2.3 as L rises: a stronger
model is confident sooner, so it commits more per pass and needs fewer of them. On
the NPU applications are free and passes are not, so this is the good direction
twice over.

**L=8 is a dip, not a ceiling.** 89.3% against 89.9% at L=4 and 92.5% at L=16. On
one seed a 0.6-point dip is noise, and reading it as saturation was the mistake
recorded at the top of this file.

**Sixteen applications of a 1.05M block beat 8.40M parameters.** 92.5% against
91.4% overall, 84.0% against 82.1% on levels 0 to 6, and an exact tie on compound
predicates at 34.3%. The looped block does it with an eighth of the parameters,
which is 0.25 MB of the 4 MB memory tiles at ternary, and in fewer forward passes
than its own L=1 version. This is the strongest evidence the design has for
decision 2, and it inverts the resident-budget question: the block does not need
to fill the tiles.

## 4. What each knob does, separated

Three knobs, one table, because the plan treats two of them as interchangeable and
they are not.

| knob | costs on the NPU | worth overall | worth on compound predicates |
|---|---|---|---|
| loop applications L | nothing but on-chip time: no dispatch, no DMA | +6.8 points to L=16, still rising | 6% → 34% |
| block parameters | the 4 MB memory tiles cap them | matched by L=16 at an eighth of them | 34% at 8 layers, which L=16 ties |
| passes, via the schedule | a full block sweep each | +3.3, and a twelfth of the passes | 1% → 18% |

The cheapest knob is also the strongest one measured. Parameters buy nothing that
sixteen applications of an eighth as many do not, at least up to this size.
The schedule is nearly free to change and was worth as much as three doublings of
the loop.

The one honest deduction against the loop: applications are free of dispatch and
DMA but not of time. L=16 at 2.3 passes is about 37 block applications per program
against L=1's 4.7, so it is roughly eight times the arithmetic for its 6.8 points.
Whether that lands inside the per-turn budget is the cost model's question, and the
answer depends on the kernel measurement in step 5 rather than on anything here.

## 5. The control arm, and the cost argument the design rests on

The same one-layer block, decoded left to right instead of in parallel:

| arm | loops | block params | passes | block applications | compile | goal | levels 0-6 | level 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| control | 1 | 1.05M | 29.6 | 29.6 | 100.0% | **98.5%** | 97.1% | 85.1% |
| control | 8 | 1.05M | 29.6 | 237 | 99.6% | 96.8% | 92.9% | 73.1% |
| diffusion | 16 | 1.05M | 2.3 | 37 | 98.8% | 92.5% | 84.0% | 34.3% |

Three things, and the third is the one to take to the owner.

**The loop helps the parallel decode and hurts the causal one.** +6.8 points for
the diffusion arm from L=1 to L=16; −1.7 for the control from L=1 to L=8, and −12
on compound predicates. That is the asymmetry the design would want if it is right
about why the loop helps: resolving 64 slots jointly in one pass is the kind of
work extra depth does, while deciding one token with the prefix already committed
is not.

**One causal decoder layer nearly solves the task.** 98.5% goal, 100% compile,
85.1% on compound predicates, from 1.05M parameters in the block — the same
quality as step 1's four-layer control. Whatever the structural binding did, it
left very little for the decoder to do.

**The parallel decode's cost advantage does not survive the loop count it needs.**
The plan's cost model compares "one token at a time, 30 passes" against "8 slots at
a time, 4 passes" at the same loop count, and the second row is 4 to 6 times
cheaper. But the measured configurations are not at the same loop count: the
control needs L=1 and the diffusion arm needs L=16 to reach its best. Counted in
block applications per program — the thing a resident model actually spends, since
the weights never move — that is 29.6 against 37. The parallel decode is not
cheaper. It is 6 points worse and costs a quarter more arithmetic.

One thing makes that worse and one thing is what the parallel decode has left. The
sampler here decodes the whole canvas on every pass for both arms, so an
incremental causal decode with a key-value cache would be *cheaper still*. And the
parallel decode does not have a shorter dependency chain, as an earlier draft of
this section claimed: its sixteen loop iterations are sequential too, so it is 37
dependent block applications against the control's 30. What it does have is the
ability to revise a slot it has already written, which the causal arm cannot, and
one more thing that section 5b measures.

On the test split, then, step 2 argues against decision 9's choice of decode:
masked diffusion was chosen partly on a cost argument that the measured loop count
reverses, and it is six points worse besides. Section 5b is why that is not the
conclusion.

### 5b. The unseen world, where the order reverses

Everything above is the test split — tasks from the same 104 worlds the model
trained on. The product's schemas come from worlds it has not. The one-layer
control had never been run on the held-out world; run now, same checkpoints, same
scorer, one seed each:

| arm | loops | passes | trained-on worlds (test) | unseen world (holdout) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| control | 1 | 29.6 | **98.5%** | 80.0% (compile 84.7%) |
| diffusion, threshold 0.99 | 16 | 2.8 | 92.5% | **90.2%** (compile 96.7%) |

On that one seed the diffusion arm is ten points ahead on the world neither model
trained on, and an earlier draft of this section said so as if it settled
something. It does not. Two more control seeds and one more diffusion configuration
later:

| unseen world | compile | goal | tool slots | field slots |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| control, seed 0 | 84.7% | 80.0% | 92.6% | 90.6% |
| control, seed 1 | 81.1% | 73.8% | 85.4% | 80.2% |
| control, seed 2 | 98.5% | **94.5%** | 98.6% | 93.0% |
| diffusion L=16, seed 0 | 96.7% | **90.2%** | 98.0% | 87.4% |
| diffusion L=16, seed 1 | 96.4% | 86.9% | — | — |
| diffusion L=16, seed 2 | 93.8% | 86.2% | — | — |
| diffusion L=32, seed 0 | 83.3% | 79.6% | 91.4% | 89.8% |
| diffusion 8 layers, seed 0 | 97.8% | 89.5% | 98.4% | 85.8% |
| diffusion 4 layers (step 1), seed 0 | 96.4% | 85.8% | 96.5% | 87.1% |

Three seeds each, at the matched configuration (one-layer block; the control at
L=1, the diffusion arm at L=16 under the confidence schedule):

| | trained-on worlds (test) | unseen world (holdout) |
|---|---|---|
| control | **97.4% ± 0.9** | 82.8% ± 10.6 (74 to 95) |
| diffusion | 92.1% ± 0.4 | **87.8% ± 2.1** (86 to 90) |

On the test split the control is five points ahead, reliably. On the unseen world
the diffusion arm's mean is five points ahead, and with three seeds and the
control's spread that difference is not statistically established (Welch t ≈ 0.8).
What *is* established is the spread: every diffusion seed lands between 86 and 90,
while the control lands anywhere from 74 to 95. The diffusion arm's worst seed
beats the control's mean. Neither arm reliably wins on the unseen world on the
mean; the diffusion arm reliably does not lose badly there, and the control
sometimes does.

What the first table shows is *what fails* when a run fails there: the tool-slot
column. Goal success tracks tool-slot accuracy almost exactly, in both arms, and a
wrong tool is a compile error, so the bad runs are the ones whose **binding did not
transfer** — the same failure R7 found in its seed 2. The binding lives in the
encoder and the pointer head, which the two arms share. The decode is not what
decides whether a run generalises; the encoder draw is.

So the honest statement of step 2's decode comparison, after three seeds of each:
**on worlds the model trained on, the causal arm is five points better, reliably.
On a world it did not, the diffusion arm is the reliable one — three seeds within
four points of each other, none below 86 — while the control's three seeds span
twenty-one points, and its mean is five points lower.** The failures on the unseen
world are binding failures in both arms, so the encoder is where the variance
comes from; why the diffusion arm's binding transfers more consistently with the
same encoder is not measured. One plausible reason: the masked-diffusion objective
makes the model resolve every slot from partial context under random masking,
which exercises the schema-vector pathway far harder than teacher-forced
left-to-right prediction does, and leaves less room to memorise token sequences.
That is a hypothesis.

For decision 9's choice of decode this is: the cost argument does not hold as
written (section 5), the test-split accuracy argument goes against it by five
points, and the unseen-world reliability argument goes for it. A planner ships to
schemas it never trained on, so the last of those is the one that should weigh
most, and it is the one that was never in the plan.

What would move this further: anything that stabilises binding transfer, which is
now the highest-value experiment in the track regardless of decode; and a
non-template request, which nothing here tests.

**L=32, for the loop curve.** 91.6% goal on the test split under the confidence
schedule against L=16's 92.5%, and 79.6% on the unseen world against 90.2%. The
curve turns over after 16, on this seed. Decision 9's range should read 8 to 16.

### 5c. The two cells the grid did not cover: real parameters, looped

Four and eight layers, each looped four times, seed 0, confidence schedule:

| block | loops | block params | passes | test compile | test goal | unseen compile | unseen goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 layer | 16 | 1.05M | 2.3 | 98.8% | 92.5% | 96.7% | 90.2% |
| 4 layers | 4 | 4.20M | 2.2 | 99.2% | 92.4% | 98.5% | 92.0% |
| 8 layers | 4 | 8.40M | 2.1 | 99.3% | 92.3% | 99.6% | **95.3%** |
| 8 layers | 1 | 8.40M | 2.2 | 99.4% | 91.4% | 97.8% | 89.5% |

Two readings.

**The test split has a ceiling for this arm, and it is about 92.5%.** Sixteen
applications of one layer, four of four, four of eight, one of eight: 92.5, 92.4,
92.3, 91.4. Spending compute as loops or as parameters reaches the same place, and
neither reaches the control's 97.4. What separates the arms on the test split is
the decode, not capacity, which is the same thing section 3 found on compound
predicates.

**On the unseen world the big looped block is the best number in the track.**
95.3% goal at 99.6% compile — above every control seed's mean and level with the
control's best draw — from a block that is 2.0 MB at ternary, half the memory
tiles. One seed, and this arm's unseen-world numbers span 80 to 95 across
configurations, so the honest claim is only that the top of that range is where
parameters and loops are both present. Seeds on this cell are the next thing to
buy.

## 6. What the plan should change

Stated as amendments rather than made, since the plan is a decision record and its
decisions are the owner's.

- **Decision 9's loop range, narrowed at the top.** "L runs 8 to 32" should read
  4 to 16: 16 is the best setting on a one-layer block and 32 is below it on both
  splits, by 0.9 on the test split and by 10.6 on the unseen world. What is amended is the word "free" — free of dispatch and DMA, and eight
  times the arithmetic. And L is not the only effort dial: the pass count is a
  second one, cheaper per point on the hardest level.
- **Decision 9's K.** "K stays at 4 to 8" is the opposite of what the data wants.
  A confidence-threshold schedule with a cap of 32 spends 2.7 passes on average
  and is better than a fixed 32; a fixed 8 is 3.3 points worse than either. The
  cap matters, not the count, and decision 8 already keeps the whole schedule in
  the kernel so a cap costs nothing when it is not reached.
- **Decision 8's mask, one correction.** The grammar mask has to be applied per
  committed slot, not once per denoising pass. Applied per pass it changes nothing
  at all, because the slots committed together never see each other
  (`results/R7.md` section 3).
- **Decision 14's block shape.** "Four to six layers at width 384 to 512, looped
  8 to 32 times" is close to what the measurement points at, with the loop count
  read as 4 to 16. On the test split the block's size does not matter past 1M
  parameters; on the unseen world the biggest looped block measured (8 layers,
  4 loops, 2 MB at ternary) is the best number in the track at 95.3%, one seed.
  So the shape stands, and the reason for it is generalisation rather than
  test-split accuracy, which the plan did not anticipate.
- **Decision 2 is the decision this step supports most.** "Looping is the whole
  game on a resident block" is what the equal-compute rows say at every rung, and
  L=16 beating 8x the parameters is the strongest form of it. It also pays a second
  time by lowering the pass count, which decision 2 did not claim.
