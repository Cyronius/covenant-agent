# R20: a real LLM chooses tools far better than the tiny planner, but doesn't know our program language

**Date:** 2026-09-28. **Branch:** `description-reading`. **Spend:** about
$5.60 on Cerebras' API (Qwen3.8-27B, pay-as-you-go), $0 on pods.
**Plan:** `.claude/plans/llm-baseline.md`.
**Picks up:** the owner's question after R19: "a normal LLM would be able to
reason about this and make a good tool choice. Are we sure this is just data?
This model seems very weak."

## The result

1. **The 27B makes the tool choices the tiny planner gets wrong.**
   - **Lookalike tools** (fetch the spot or fetch the advertiser?): it calls
     the other thing's tool instead of the right one 1.7% of the time, and
     never when it reasons first. A0 does it 8.1%, SPt with S6 rows 9–11%.
   - **Decoys the request can separate:** the 27B picks the decoy 0–3.5% of
     the time. A0 and SPt without S6 rows pick it 44–54% of the time. That is
     a coin toss: they don't read decoy descriptions at all. SPt with 50% S6
     rows gets it down to 8%.
2. **It even handles most of the decoys I called undecidable.** On list and
   fetch decoys whose request never names the difference, the 27B picks a
   decoy only 7–13% of the time, against 37–40% for A0.
   - Ordinary language sense tells it which tool is the normal one ("the
     front desk board", not "the restaurant booking board").
   - So those rows are not unanswerable for a model that knows English. The
     decoyed exam is not unfair; the tiny model lacks the language.
   - This corrects 2026-09-28's reading in
     `.claude/plans/decidable-decoys.md`.
3. **The 27B loses whole tasks because it doesn't know our program
   language.**
   - Answering directly, it scores 50.7% on the plain exam against A0's
     74.3% on the same tasks.
   - Of its failures, 57 don't compile even after two repair rounds, 17 fail
     at run time, and 57 run but get the wrong result.
   - The failures are wrong types, unparseable lines, code after `STOP`, and
     different program shapes, not wrong tools.
   - Allowed to reason first, it scores 74.0% on plain, level with A0.
4. **On the harder exams the 27B wins outright, even answering directly.**

   | exam | 27B direct | 27B reasoning | best tiny planner |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | decoyed | 42.3% | 62.0% | 44.0% (SPt 50% S6) |
   | flip | 46.0% | — | 32.0% (SPt 50% S6) |

5. **Answer to the owner's question:** yes, the tiny model is weak at
   reading; it isn't only the data.
   - The data explains the collapse from 89% to 54% (R19).
   - It does not explain why A0 treats decoys as a coin toss when a general
     model barely hesitates. That is the missing language knowledge.
   - A0's one real advantage is that it was drilled on 30,000 examples of our
     program format, and the 27B saw one.

## Terms

- **27B:** Qwen3.8-27B on Cerebras, off the shelf.
  - Our standard instructions and one worked example, temperature 0.
  - No grammar: the API can't take one, so it writes programs freely.
  - Up to two repair rounds, where the typechecker's errors are fed back.
- **Direct / reasoning first:** reasoning off, or the model's own reasoning
  on at "low" effort before it writes the program (about 800 tokens a task).
  The reasoning arm ran on 150 plain and 150 decoyed tasks only.
- **A0, SPt:** the tiny planners, as in R14–R19. Their programs are the ones
  generated in R14–R17, cut to the same tasks.
- **Lookalike, decoy:** as R18–R19.
  - A lookalike is a same-shape tool for another thing.
  - A decoy is a same-signature tool for the same thing, with a different
    job.
  - **Decidable decoys** are the ones where the request's wording fits the
    right tool and no decoy (`results/logs/decoy_decidable.py`). This covers
    the four flip slots (delete, flag, re-parent, send) only.
- **Swapped:** the program called the lookalike or a decoy *and not* the
  right tool. The rival must not be one the reference calls for its own
  purpose.
  - This is the fair test of tool choice for the 27B.
  - A plain "did it call the right tool" test counts a different but valid
    program shape against it, such as listing and filtering instead of
    fetching by id. The 27B writes such shapes because it never saw our
    program style.

## 1. Numbers

`results/logs/llm_baseline.py report` → `results/logs/llm_baseline/report.md`.
There are 300 tasks per exam, drawn once (`llm_baseline/ids.json`). The plain
and decoyed exams use the same 300 base tasks.

**Plain exam**

| model | goal | compiles | swapped: lookalike | called: lookalike |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 27B, direct | 50.7% | 81.0% | **1.7%** | 77.5% |
| 27B, reasoning first (150) | 74.0% | 93.3% | **0.0%** | 71.8% |
| A0 options-off | 74.3% | 86.0% | 8.1% | 88.9% |
| A0 options-off + backoff | 82.3% | 95.3% | 0.3% | 98.0% |
| SPt options-off | 66.0% | 81.0% | 4.4% | 86.2% |
| SPt 25% / 50% S6 | 53.3% / 53.7% | 61.7% / 60.7% | 9.1% / 10.7% | 63.4% / 61.1% |

**Decoyed exam**

| model | goal | swapped: decoy, decidable | swapped: decoy, undecidable |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27B, direct | 42.3% | **3.5%** | **12.8%** |
| 27B, reasoning first (150) | 62.0% | **0.0%** | **7.4%** |
| A0 options-off | 29.3% | 49.6% | 39.7% |
| SPt options-off | 38.7% | 44.3% | 23.8% |
| SPt 25% / 50% S6 | 42.7% / 44.0% | 16.5% / 7.8% | 10.3% / 8.9% |

**Flip probe**

| model | goal | swapped: decoy, decidable | swapped: decoy, undecidable |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27B, direct | 46.0% | **0.4%** | **10.5%** |
| A0 options-off | 17.3% | 49.6% | 37.0% |
| SPt options-off | 17.0% | 53.7% | 18.7% |
| SPt 25% / 50% S6 | 30.3% / 32.0% | 22.1% / 7.8% | 6.2% / 7.9% |

- **The 27B's "called" rate is lower than its swap rate suggests,** for
  example 77.5% on plain lookalikes. The gap is program shape, not wrong
  tools. On the decoyed exam it declines with ABORT about as often as on
  plain (36 and 32 tasks), so refusing is not what fills the gap.
- **Backoff makes A0's lookalike choices as good as the 27B's** (0.3% swaps).
  Backoff uses the typechecker to reject non-compiling swaps. It cannot help
  with decoys, which compile.
- **SPt with S6 rows closes much of the decoy gap** (8% swaps against the
  27B's 0–4%). It pays for it with lookalike swaps on plain (9–11%), as R17
  and R18 found.

## 2. Checks

- **Scorer agreement** (`llm_baseline.py agree`):
  - every reference program on the 900 tasks passes the harness scorer used
    for the 27B;
  - on 50 of A0's plain programs, that scorer and `evaluate.py --score` give
    the same verdict 50 times out of 50.
- **The same measures for every model.** Tool choice is read from the program
  text for all of them.
- **A0's "called: lookalike" on these 300 tasks is 88.9%,** against R18's
  teacher-forced 89%. That is a sanity check on the sample and the script.
- **Paused tasks are scored slightly differently.**
  - The tiny planners' second segments were generated from the reference's
    registers and scored segment by segment (a task counts only if every
    segment passes).
  - The 27B ran the whole loop on its own registers.
  - Paused tasks are about a tenth of each exam.

## 3. What this changes

- **For the owner's question:** the evidence is that language knowledge,
  not more careful data, is what separates the tiny planner from a model
  that "reasons about" tool choice. A general model reads decoy descriptions
  almost perfectly with no training on them. Our tiny planners without decoy
  training can't read them at all.
- **For the decidable-decoys plan (on hold):** its premise was that list and
  fetch decoys are unanswerable. The 27B shows they are mostly answerable with
  ordinary language sense. For a tiny model trained from scratch they may
  still act as noise, so the cause test in that plan still makes sense. But
  "remove the undecidable decoys" is no longer obviously right; those are
  what a real LLM gets right and ours doesn't.
- **The format gap is the easier half.** The 27B's whole-task losses are
  compile errors and unfamiliar program shapes. That is what fine-tuning on
  our rows fixes, and what the tiny planners already have.

## Next (for the owner to choose)

1. **Pretrained language knowledge plus our format.**
   - Fine-tune a small pretrained model on the S6 rows and score it on these
     same 900 tasks, with `llm_baseline.py report` as is.
   - The v1 track's Qwen 0.8B is the obvious candidate.
   - If it keeps the 27B's tool choice and gains A0's fluency, that beats
     every tiny planner.
   - The size budget is the question: 0.8B is about 100 times A0.
2. **Give the tiny planner a pretrained reader.**
   - Put the 33M bge-small encoder, or a distilled piece of it, in front of
     the planner for descriptions.
   - SPt's stages were distilled from it and already read at 89–92% in
     isolation (R11–R12). The planner doesn't use that reading well on
     decoys.
   - Cheaper in size, less certain.
3. **Cheap extras on Cerebras if wanted:**
   - the 27B with a few worked examples, to see how much of its format gap
     three examples close (about $2);
   - `gpt-oss-120b`, the other model on the account, for a second opinion
     (about $3).
